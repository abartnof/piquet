# Piquet

**Piquet** (English `/pɪˈkɛt/`, French `[pikɛ]`) is a two-player trick-and-meld
card game played with a 32-card pack. First recorded in France in the early
sixteenth century, it was for roughly three hundred years the most prestigious
card game in Europe — delegates to a card congress in Vienna in 1892 voted it
the most "classic" of all card games — before falling out of fashion after the
First World War. David Parlett calls it "a classic game of relatively great
antiquity… still one of the most skill-rewarding card games for two."

Its distinctive feature is that the two players first *declare* combinations
from their hands for points, and only then play out twelve tricks. Because every
declaration is truthful and public, declaring buys points at the price of
telling your opponent what you hold. Cavendish, the game's Victorian codifier,
put the resulting tension plainly: a player "puts up with the loss of several
points in calling his hand, on the chance of afterwards dividing or winning the
cards."

## History

### Origins

The game appears as *Le Cent* — "the hundred", after its target score — in
Rabelais's *Gargantua and Pantagruel* in 1535, listed among the hero's
pastimes. Legend attributes its invention to Étienne de Vignolles ("La Hire"), a
companion of Joan of Arc, though a Spanish origin is equally plausible: the words
*pique* and *repique* appear to derive from Spanish, and the game was known in
England as "cent" after the Spanish *cientos*.

It reached England twice over. Mary I's marriage to Philip II of Spain in 1554
brought the Spanish form; Charles I's marriage to Henrietta Maria of France in
1625 brought the French name, which stuck. The game was popular enough in
Germany during the Thirty Years' War to be the subject of a 1634 poem,
*Allamodisch Picket Spiel*.

A French literature existed by 1649, when *Le Second ieu du picquet de la cour*
was printed. The earliest surviving English rules are Charles Cotton's, in *The
Compleat Gamester* of 1674.

### The thirty-six card game

Cotton's game is recognisably piquet but not the modern one. The pack is
**thirty-six** cards — "throw out of the Pack the Deuces, Treys, Fours and
Fives… which are in number thirty and six" — twelve are dealt to each player and
**twelve** left as a stock, from which elder "may take in eight of the twelve."
The set is "an hundred."

Two further differences are substantial. Point, then called the **ruff**, was
scored by pip value rather than length, at one point per ten pips with a rounding
rule. And tricks were scored by high card rather than by trick: "for every Ace,
King, Queen, Knave, or Ten, he reckons one."

Much else was already fixed. Sequences scored 3, 4, then **15** for a quint and
16 for a sixième — the peculiar jump already in place in 1674. Trios scored 3 and
quatorzes 14. Capot was 40 and the cards 10. Pique and repique already carried
their modern values, expressed as the score leaping to sixty and ninety rather
than as bonuses — a convention French and German players still use.

### The thirty-two card game

By the time Edmond Hoyle published *A Short Treatise on the Game of Piquet* in
1744, the modern game had arrived. Hoyle computes the chances of "taking in any
one, two, three, four, or five certain Cards", and a five-card draw is the modern
elder exchange. The pack had been cut to thirty-two and the stock to eight. The
transition therefore falls between 1674 and 1744.

Hoyle's treatise is essentially a work of applied probability, aimed at the
hardest decision in the game: what to throw away. It ran through many editions
and was folded into the collected *Hoyle's Games Improved*, which kept the rules
stable across the eighteenth century.

### Codification and decline

The English club game was fixed late in the nineteenth century by the Portland
and Turf Clubs, whose laws were published with a treatise by **Cavendish** (Henry
Jones, 1831–99). Cavendish's numbered laws remain the authority behind every
modern description of the game.

Piquet declined sharply in the twentieth century. Britannica, in an entry written
by Parlett, suggests it was hurt by being "regarded as essentially aristocratic
and upper-class" and by taking "much explaining"; Wikipedia blames the rise of
"Gin Rummy and other lowbrow games that are easier to learn and faster to play."
Both are probably right. Piquet rewards study, and its scoring is intricate
enough that a beginner cannot simply be dealt in.

## The pack and the deal

Thirty-two cards: ace, king, queen, jack, ten, nine, eight and seven in each
suit, ranking in that order with the ace high. For tie-breaking, aces count 11,
court cards 10, and the rest at face value.

Players cut for the deal, and the winner may choose who deals first; the deal
then alternates. **Dealing is a disadvantage.** The non-dealer is the **elder
hand**, the dealer the **younger hand**.

Each player receives twelve cards, dealt in twos or threes — the dealer must
declare which and keep to it for the rest of the partie. The remaining eight
cards form the **talon**, laid face down in two packets: five for elder, three for
younger.

A **partie** is six deals.

## Carte blanche

A hand containing no court card — no king, queen or jack — is a **carte
blanche** and scores 10. It must be announced as soon as it is noticed and proved
by dealing the hand rapidly face up.

The proof is timed so as not to leak information: the holder shows *after* the
opponent has discarded but *before* discarding themselves. If elder holds it, he
first announces how many cards he intends to discard, so that younger can choose
her own discards before seeing his hand.

Carte blanche occurs about once in **1,792 hands** -- so, since a deal has
two hands, about once in 896 deals. Two players cannot hold it
simultaneously in the 32-card game: that would require twenty-four cards with no
court among them, and only twenty exist. In Cotton's 36-card game it was
possible, and younger's blank cancelled elder's pique.

## The exchange

Elder discards between one and five cards face down and draws the same number
from the top five of the talon. If he takes fewer than five, he may look at the
remainder of his five — so **elder always knows all five of his talon cards**.

Younger then discards at least one card and at most the number elder left
untaken, usually three. If she leaves any, she may expose them to both players
after elder leads, or leave them face down for neither to see.

Both players keep their own discards beside them and may consult them during
play.

Britannica notes that elder "in practice usually exchanges five cards", and
Cotton had already observed in 1674 that "it is no small advantage to the eldest
to have the benefit of discarding."

## Declarations

Three categories are contested in a fixed order — **point**, **sequence**,
**set** — and each is a small dialogue. Elder announces his best holding in the
category; younger replies **"good"** (elder's is better), **"not good"** (hers
is), or **"equal"**. Only then does the next category begin, so elder may adapt a
later declaration to what he has learned. He may not revise a declaration upward
once answered.

Elder scores what he has won as he goes, then leads to the first trick, scoring
one for the lead. Only then does younger declare and score the categories she
called "not good".

**Point** goes to the player with the most cards in a single suit, and scores
that number of cards. Ties are broken by the summed pip value of the suit; if
still equal, neither scores.

**Sequence** goes to the longest run of three or more cards in one suit. A run of
three is a *tierce*, of four a *quart*, then *quint*, *sixième*, *septième*,
*huitième*. They score 3, 4, 15, 16, 17 and 18 — that is, the length, until five
cards, after which a bonus of ten applies. Ties are broken by the top card; if
still equal, neither scores. The winner also scores every other sequence he
holds; the loser scores nothing at all.

**Set** means three or four cards of the same rank, ten or higher; nines and
below never count. Three of a kind is a *trio* and scores 3; four is a
*quatorze* and scores 14. Any quatorze beats any trio, and higher ranks beat
lower. Ties are impossible — two players cannot both hold three of a rank. As
with sequences, the winner scores his lesser sets and the loser scores none.

## Sinking

A player is never obliged to declare. Withholding a combination — or declaring a
smaller one than you hold — is called **sinking**, and it is the strategic heart
of the game.

Cavendish's treatment is the classic one. He describes sinking as a manoeuvre to
be guarded against, gives its rationale, and notes when it is most attractive:
"It is especially resorted to when a player has a suit unguarded, and calling all
he holds would expose the fact."

Crucially, his examples are all of *partial* concealment rather than silence: a
player "declares five spades, when he might have six"; holds a quart to the knave
but calls only a tierce to the knave; holds a quatorze but calls only a trio. A
player may declare any value at or below what he actually holds.

Cavendish also teaches reading the opponent rather than the cards alone. "Your
adversary, for instance, is a player who rarely discards from his point. He calls
five cards, and declares five spades, when he might have six. You should
immediately suspect that he may be sinking a card of his point." His treatise
includes a section titled *Habit of Adversary*.

The cost of sinking is absolute and ancient. Cotton, in 1674, ruled that a player
"that sheweth not his Ruff before he play his first Card" loses it "absolutely".

## The play

Twelve tricks are played without trumps. The player leading may lead anything;
the other must follow suit if able, and otherwise may play any card. The higher
card of the suit led wins, and the winner leads to the next trick.

Each player scores **one for every card he leads, and one for every trick he wins
with the second card**. The winner of the last trick scores two instead of one.

At the end of play, the player who has taken more than six tricks scores **10 for
the cards**; at six each, nobody does. A player taking all twelve scores **40 for
capot** instead.

## Pique and repique

Two bonuses reward overwhelming hands. Both depend on reaching thirty points
before the opponent has scored anything at all, and both depend on a fixed
reckoning order. Cavendish's Law 67 gives it:

> I. Carte blanche · II. Point · III. Sequences · IV. Quatorzes and trios ·
> V. Points made in play · VI. The cards

**Repique** is thirty reached "in his hand alone" — in declarations only, before
a card is played — and adds **60**.

**Pique** is thirty reached "in hand and play" before the opponent has reckoned
anything, and adds **30**. Only elder can score a pique, because he scores one
for leading to the first trick before younger has declared anything.

A player scores one or the other, never both. Equality in a category does not
count as scoring and so does not block either bonus.

Two consequences follow from the ordering. A carte blanche, reckoning first,
both earns its holder a pique and **denies one to the opponent** — Cotton's
"his Blank shall hinder the other Picq and Repicq, although he hath nothing to
shew but his Blank". And the ten for cards, reckoning last, can never contribute
to a pique. Cavendish is explicit: "A capot reckons after points made in play;
and, therefore, does not count toward a pique."

Parlett gives a worked repique: 7 for point, then 15, 4 and 3 for sequences, then
3 for a trio, making 32 — plus 60, for 92. Had younger held a carte blanche, she
would have scored first and the repique would have been denied.

The example is arithmetic rather than a real deal: exhaustive search shows no
twelve-card hand can hold a point of seven, sequences scoring 15, 4 and 3, and a
trio at once. A point of seven forces a seven-card suit, which leaves the other
five cards confined to a single suit, and a trio needs three suits.

## Scoring the partie

Under **rubicon** scoring, all six deals are played out regardless of the running
score, and the settlement is computed at the end.

- If the loser reached 100, the winner scores **the difference plus 100**.
- If the loser failed to reach 100 — they are **rubiconed** — the winner scores
  **the sum of both scores plus 100**, whether or not the winner reached 100
  themselves.

So 105 to 101 pays 104, while 97 to 89 pays 286. Failing to reach a hundred is
punished far more severely than losing narrowly, which shapes how a trailing
player must gamble late in a partie.

If the scores are level after six deals, each player deals once more; the partie
then concludes even if the scores are level again.

## Strategy

The oldest advice concerns the discard, and Hoyle devoted an entire treatise to
it in 1744. The modern consensus is to shed nines and below even at the cost of
the point, on the grounds that point is the cheapest category — and that a fifth
card in a sequence is worth eleven more points than the fourth, so a quint is
worth wrecking a suit for.

In the play, the standard device is the **stop card** — a guard held in the
opponent's long suit to break a run of tricks — and the **tenace**, a holding
such as ace-queen that wins if the opponent leads the suit but not if you do.

Above all, the game is about the exchange rate between points and information.
Every declaration is truthful, so every declaration narrows what your opponent
must guess. By the time the first card is led, a player who has declared fully
may have told the opponent the shape of most of the hand.

## Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Elder hand** | The non-dealer, who exchanges and leads first |
| **Younger hand** | The dealer, at a disadvantage |
| **Talon** | The eight undealt cards, split five and three |
| **Écart** | The discard, or the act of discarding |
| **Carte blanche** | A hand with no court cards; 10 points |
| **Carte rouge** | Continental only: a hand every card of which scores. 10, 20, 40 or 50 depending on the source; absent from the English club game |
| **Point** | The longest suit; the German *Rummel*, Cotton's *ruff* |
| **Tierce, quart, quint, sixième, septième, huitième** | Sequences of 3 to 8 cards: 3, 4, 15, 16, 17, 18 |
| **Tierce major** | A sequence headed by the ace |
| **Trio** (*brelan*, *gedritt*) | Three of a rank, ten or higher; 3 points |
| **Quatorze** (*geviert*) | Four of a rank, ten or higher; 14 points |
| **Sinking** | Withholding or understating a declaration |
| **Pique** | 30 in hand and play before the opponent scores; +30 |
| **Repique** | 30 in declarations alone before the opponent scores; +60 |
| **Cards** | Winning more than six tricks; 10 points |
| **Capot** | Winning all twelve tricks; 40 points |
| **Rubicon** | Failing to reach 100 in a partie, heavily penalised |
| **Partie** | A match of six deals |

## Variants

**Piquet au Cent** is the older race-to-100 form, and differs enough to count as
a separate game: Cavendish's laws for it allow younger to stand pat without
discarding, and some descriptions use a 36-card pack.

**Piquet Normand** (three players), **Piquet Voleur** (four) and *Piquet à
Écrire* are French multiplayer adaptations.

**Auction Piquet**, developed by British prisoners of war between 1914 and 1918,
allows players to bid for the elder position.

Parlett has published his own **Contract Piquet**, and continental play preserves
local variations: German rules give the last trick three points and capot thirty;
French rules score the last trick as two and, unusually, let both players score
their combinations rather than only the category winner. The Comtat Venaissin
had a house rule called *Estachin* under which peeking at an opponent's cards
cost seventy points.

## An unresolved puzzle

Asked in 1744 for "the highest Number to be made of a Repique and Capot", Hoyle
answered **170 points**. Working from the modern rules, the best hand we can
construct — a point of eight, a huitième for 18, and a quatorze for 14, giving 40
in declarations, plus 60 for repique, twelve leads, the last trick and 40 for
capot — reaches only **153**.

The discrepancy is unexplained. It may reflect a scoring difference in Hoyle's
day, an error in our reasoning, or an error in the optical transcription of a
280-year-old book.
